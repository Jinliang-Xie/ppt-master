__From rated performance to lifetime service in hydrogen PEM fuel\-cell systems__

# __Abstract__

Hydrogen proton\-exchange\-membrane fuel\-cell (PEMFC) systems are usually judged by rated efficiency, power density, range and stack life. These measures describe a system when new; they do not reveal the service it delivers through changing load, interrupted fuel supply, maintenance and ageing. We synthesize hydrogen supply, duty\-specific operation, degradation, downtime and replacement as a single lifetime story. Mobile systems compress transients and refuelling dependence into crowded working days; backup systems wait through long calendar time for rare moments of demand; stationary and combined\-heat\-and\-power systems accumulate operating hours, fuel use and heat–power trade\-offs. Across these duties, the literature is rich in initial specifications and terminal durability values but sparse in records that connect exposure, state change, intervention and delivered service on the same asset. This missing middle changes how PEMFC advantage should be judged: supply sets the starting position, duty writes the operating history, and that history determines whether an initial advantage survives, narrows or disappears before retirement. Durability is therefore best read as a service biography rather than a stack rating.

# __1. From rated performance to service duty__

A fuel\-cell stack can meet every factory specification and still fail to deliver the service for which the surrounding system was purchased. Rated power says how much electrical output is available at a defined operating point. Efficiency relates an energy input to an output under stated conditions. Power density describes compactness. A durability target specifies a test or design horizon. None of these quantities alone records whether a vehicle completed its routes, whether a protected load remained energized, or whether a stationary plant supplied electricity and useful heat when required. The difference is not semantic. It changes the object being assessed from a conversion device to a service system.

Hydrogen\-system reviews explain production pathways and infrastructure; fuel\-cell reviews explain materials, mechanisms and durability; application and lifecycle studies compare sectors and burdens. Yet these literatures are usually joined through assumed efficiencies, lifetimes or replacement intervals. The missing synthesis is the life between those assumptions: how a named duty creates a pattern of load, start–stop, standby and maintenance, and how that pattern becomes degradation, interruption and replacement. This Review follows that life from delivered hydrogen to service before retirement.

Lifetime service is the service an asset actually completes before retirement. For mobility it is transport completed; for backup it is critical load protected when demand arrives; for stationary and CHP systems it is accepted electricity and useful heat. These outcomes share a logic but not a common unit.

Consider three systems at the start of life. At a depot before dawn, a fuel\-cell bus or truck is fuelled for a long working day. Its value lies in completing a scheduled duty with little time available for repair. In a critical facility, a backup unit sits quiet while controls, stored hydrogen and auxiliary systems wait for an uncertain outage. Its low utilization is intentional, but one failed start can dominate its service record. At a building or industrial site, a stationary PEMFC runs for long periods and may be valued for both power and useful heat. The hardware family is similar. The clocks that matter, the exposure accumulated and the cost of interruption are not.

The same hardware can therefore live on different clocks. Mobile duty accumulates transients and refuelling events; backup duty accumulates calendar exposure and rare starts; stationary duty accumulates operating hours and heat–power demand. Calendar age, operating hours and event counts describe those lives, while completed transport, protected\-load events and accepted electricity and heat reveal their value. Utilization gains meaning only inside the duty it serves.

Service definition also determines the system boundary. A mobile PEMFC cannot deliver a trip without accessible hydrogen, dispensing, controls, cooling and a vehicle that is otherwise available. A backup stack cannot protect a load without fuel inventory, start power, ventilation, switching and a repair or fallback plan. A CHP unit cannot deliver its intended combined service if heat is produced when no useful demand exists. The relevant system contains the direct auxiliaries and supply interfaces that decide whether stack output becomes service. It need not contain every part of the hydrogen economy.

Rated efficiency, power density, start performance and catalyst loading retain their importance, but they become explanatory variables rather than final verdicts. The analytical movement is from starting conditions to exposure, from exposure to changing state, and from changing state to completed or missed service (Fig. 1).

These observations lead to the Review's central question: how do hydrogen supply and service duty write a lived history of degradation, interruption, maintenance and replacement, and when does that history change the value initially promised by the stack?

# __2. Hydrogen supply sets the starting conditions—and can return later__

Hydrogen reaches a PEMFC with a history already attached. Production route determines much of its lifecycle carbon and water context, while purification, compression, storage and delivery determine energy loss, cost and exposure to interruption \[1–7,30,31\]. These conditions set the terrain at commissioning, but they do not then disappear. Purity, pressure, stored inventory, access and delivery can return throughout life as disturbances that alter service even while the stack remains healthy.

At commissioning, the relevant supply attributes are carbon and water intensity, purity, pressure, conditioning loss, usable reserve and accessibility. Production route, temporal matching, carrier conversion, distance and infrastructure utilization shape those attributes \[1,3–7,31–46\]. They also create equipment and dependencies that can later interrupt duty. A delayed delivery, depleted store, off\-specification fuel or unavailable dispenser can remove service while the stack remains healthy.

Supply reliability changes meaning across the three duties. The high\-utilization mobile depot needs enough hydrogen at the right pressure and time to avoid missed departures. A delayed truck or unavailable dispenser can remove service even when the vehicle stack is healthy. The backup installation needs stored fuel of adequate quantity and condition when an outage occurs, perhaps after a long quiet interval. The continuous plant needs sustained flow and may expose an upstream interruption immediately. The same annual hydrogen volume can therefore support three very different service records.

The mobile scene makes throughput visible. A busy depot may use infrastructure efficiently, yet a small maintenance margin can propagate a supply interruption across many scheduled journeys. A lightly used public station may have adequate nameplate capacity but poor local service because equipment is unavailable or too distant. California's dated network record demonstrates why capacity, utilization, availability, stoppage and access should be reported separately \[28\]. None alone diagnoses the cause. Together they prevent an installed\-capacity claim from standing in for mobility.

The backup scene reverses the intuition. Low throughput does not imply low importance. Hydrogen may remain stored for an event that rarely occurs, so annual conversion efficiency says little about success during that event. The relevant chain includes fuel inventory, storage losses, valve and control readiness, auxiliary power, start success and resupply under disrupted conditions. No record in the assembled backup set quantifies this complete chain for a PEMFC cohort. The open span cannot be filled by transferring a mobile or continuous\-duty lifetime.

Continuous stationary duty creates a third balance. Reliable supply can support high equipment utilization and make gradual efficiency changes economically visible. On\-site electrolysis may reduce transport dependence but adds a coupled production asset. Delivered hydrogen may reduce local equipment while retaining dependence on a network. The most credible configuration depends on site demand, resource quality, storage, maintenance capability and fallback. No universal movement from local to regional or corridor supply follows from these contrasting studies \[8,43–53\].

Purity, pressure, temperature and humidity can alter what the PEMFC receives and how it ages. Few studies connect these supply conditions to long\-term service under a named duty, even though a supply failure and a healthy\-stack interruption can produce the same missed trip, failed start or lost output through different mechanisms.

Supply conditions also define environmental baselines. Upstream production commonly dominates the climate result of hydrogen use, while water significance varies strongly by region and boundary \[1–4,34\]. Delivery adds energy use and, for some pathways, leakage or carrier\-conversion burdens \[36,38–41,44,54,55\]. A stack that maintains its initial efficiency cannot erase these starting burdens. Conversely, a low\-carbon supply does not guarantee good service if delivery is unreliable. Environmental credibility and operational service are distinct outcomes that share part of the same upstream history.

Over time, efficiency loss, auxiliary demand and operation away from the preferred range can increase the hydrogen required for the same service. A rise in hydrogen use per trip or kilowatt\-hour therefore carries a history; it should not be attributed to the stack before supply and utilization are separated.

Fallback changes the interpretation again. A depot may substitute vehicles, a critical facility may use batteries or a generator during start and repair, and a stationary plant may draw from the grid. Fallback can preserve service while concealing PEMFC downtime in an outcome\-only record. It also adds cost and lifecycle burden. Lifetime service should report both the protected outcome and the fraction supplied by the PEMFC chain. This preserves the reason a redundant system exists without granting the primary device service it did not deliver.

Hydrogen supply thus opens the biography and can interrupt it later. Duty determines whether that interruption becomes a missed departure, an unprotected outage or lost electricity and heat.

# __3. Service duty creates an operating history__

A duty is not an application name. It is a pattern of demanded power, starts, stops, idle periods, environment, refuelling or supply events, auxiliary use and maintenance opportunities. Repeated through time, that pattern becomes an operating history. The history is the missing middle between a new stack and a lifetime claim. It records not only hours operated, but how those hours were accumulated and what happened between them.

The high\-frequency mobile history is crowded. Power demand changes as a vehicle accelerates, climbs, cruises and stops. Refuelling and dispatch impose time constraints. Daily operation can accumulate hours quickly, while weekends, seasonal temperature changes and route reassignment add variation. A useful dataset would pair these exposures with completed duty, hydrogen use, maintenance events and system state. Earlier hydrogen studies establish mobile range, refuelling, payload and lifecycle comparisons \[11–15,56\]. Field studies now add parts of the missing operating record, although no single source closes the full chain.

Road evidence demonstrates why laboratory hours are not enough. A remote\-monitoring study of more than 100 fuel\-cell vehicles analysed long\-term operation in a real road environment and described a non\-linear, bathtub\-shaped voltage\-degradation pattern with early, steady and accelerated phases \[57\]. The analysis related road operation to start\-stop, load cycling and high\-potential idling. It provides direct field evidence that degradation rate changes across the observed life. It does not by itself convert voltage change into missed trips, maintenance or replacement.

A Chinese plug\-in hybrid fuel\-cell city\-bus study similarly treated road\-condition stack degradation as a system\-level service\-life problem rather than extrapolating from a single cell \[58\]. Earlier urban transit work documented operation and maintenance of a fuel\-cell and battery series\-hybrid bus \[59\], while a worldwide bus assessment defined roadcalls as in\-service failures that remove or replace a bus on route \[60\]. Together these sources cover exposure, system degradation and a service\-facing failure concept. Their cohorts, architectures and endpoints remain too different to splice into one quantitative lifetime curve.

Laboratory synthesis helps interpret this field pattern without replacing it. An automotive degradation review identifies dynamic load as a severe durability condition because potential, gas supply, temperature, pressure and water state change together \[61\]. It also warns that driving\-cycle tests combine load change with start\-stop, idling and overload, so the effect of one exposure is difficult to isolate. The mobile ribbon therefore places controlled stress tests beneath, not on top of, the road record.

Return to the depot scene after several months. Two vehicles can show similar odometer readings and still have different operating histories because routes, loads, starts, ambient conditions and maintenance windows differ. A mean fleet efficiency can hide a tail of vehicles that lose service disproportionately. A stack\-hour total can hide whether exposure arrived through steady operation or repeated transients. The relevant comparison is therefore among histories normalized to the service they attempted, not among application labels.

The low\-utilization backup history is sparse in operating hours and dense in elapsed time. Long standby intervals alternate with brief tests and rare demand events. A system may appear durable because it has accumulated few operating hours, yet its central requirement is readiness after calendar exposure. Conversely, repeated testing can create an operating record without representing the conditions of a real outage. Backup evidence should separate elapsed calendar time, test starts, demanded starts, successful load delivery, maintenance and fuel interventions.

Start\-up experiments show that readiness is a sequence rather than an instantaneous stack property. A backup\-oriented PEMFC stack study separated gas supply in open circuit from load connection and examined when and how the load could be applied \[62\]. This is direct experimental evidence for the start transition. It does not report years of standby or performance during an actual blackout. The backup ribbon therefore marks it as a laboratory link between readiness state and initial load, not as delivered reliability.

Real\-world telecom work moves one step closer to service. Validation of two hybrid backup configurations showed that supplementary storage covered the transient interval until the PEMFC reached operating conditions, after which load sharing shifted toward the fuel cell \[63\]. Battery and ultracapacitor configurations implemented that handoff differently. This field\-facing system evidence makes auxiliaries part of successful start. The available chunk does not supply a longitudinal failure distribution or lifetime replacement record.

High\-temperature PEM cycling at different idle temperatures provides a useful contrast: standby conditions can reshape degradation even when operating hours are low \[64\]. The broader lesson is that calendar exposure, technology and duty remain visible whenever evidence is carried from one application to another.

Continuous stationary or CHP duty accumulates hours differently. Power may be comparatively steady, but load following, heat demand and scheduled maintenance can still vary. The electrical service and useful thermal service need separate accounting because heat has value only when accepted at the right temperature and time. Stationary fuel cells can provide reliable power and integrated energy services, and published work describes monitoring and tri\-generation contexts \[17,18,65\]. Long\-duration stack and system studies add valuable trajectories, but technology and fuel boundaries must remain visible.

Reformate PEMFC stacks developed for domestic CHP achieved more than 15,000 operating hours with reported degradation rates of about 10 microvolts per hour \[66\]. This is direct long\-term stack evidence under a defined stationary context. It is more informative than a generic stationary\-life target, yet it does not report the complete building service, useful heat, downtime or replacement sequence. The stationary ribbon therefore carries the observed stack trajectory and leaves the final service segment open.

A full\-scale, production\-sized 67\-cell high\-temperature PEM stack was studied under continuous durability conditions resembling commercial stack operation \[67\]. The authors explicitly distinguished such evidence from the many small\-cell and short\-stack laboratory studies. This strengthens the general case for scale\-aware longevity testing. Its HT\-PEM technology prevents silent transfer to every low\-temperature hydrogen PEMFC, so the figure labels it as boundary evidence rather than a pooled lifetime datum.

Long\-term micro\-CHP modelling followed electrical and thermal performance over the first 15,000 hours while accounting for degradation in the HT\-PEM stack and fuel processor \[68\]. Proposed operating strategies kept thermal generation within a range or increased fuel to suppress declining electrical output. This model makes a crucial service link visible: maintaining output can shift fuel use and the heat\-power balance. It remains a model, not a field record of delivered CHP service.

At the stationary site, gradual change matters. A small efficiency loss repeated across many operating hours can increase hydrogen consumption even if no outage occurs. A planned service intervention may protect long\-term output while temporarily reducing availability. A CHP unit may continue generating electricity while failing to meet a thermal duty. These are not secondary details. They show why lifetime service has several components: amount delivered, quality, timing, reliability and the resources consumed to deliver it.

Auxiliary systems belong in every history. Compression, air supply, humidification, cooling, pumps, controls and power electronics determine whether electrochemical output becomes usable system output. Their consumption can change with operating point and age, while their failures can stop service without stack failure. The literature recognizes balance\-of\-plant components and monitoring \[17,24,69\], but does not resolve their duty\-specific contribution to downtime. Figure 3 therefore keeps stack and balance\-of\-plant histories visible as parallel contributors to the same service outcome.

Maintenance opportunity is another duty\-created exposure. A depot may concentrate service between shifts but suffer strongly when repair exceeds that window. A backup site may schedule tests and preventive service, yet access during a regional emergency may be restricted. A continuous plant can plan an outage, but every hour offline may remove electricity or heat. The same repair duration thus has different service consequences. Downtime should be weighted by demanded service, not treated as an abstract percentage alone.

Operating histories also contain interventions. Software changes, component replacement, cleaning, inspection and changes in operating strategy can alter later performance. A lifetime curve without intervention records can attribute recovery or discontinuity to the wrong cause. The correct unit is an event\-resolved history in which exposure, state measurement, intervention and service outcome share a time axis. This requirement is modest conceptually and demanding empirically.

The field already uses monitoring and machine\-learning methods for performance evaluation, durability prediction and application monitoring \[17\]. Such tools can make long records tractable, but prediction is not a substitute for a defined service endpoint. A model trained on voltage or efficiency may identify state change without telling whether a route was missed or a protected load failed. Monitoring becomes valuable for lifetime service when its target variables connect component state to system availability and delivered duty.

Figure 3 places the three duties on separate time ribbons. Each ribbon preserves its native units and evidence, then ends where the record stops before maintenance, replacement or service outcome. The visual gap is the finding: duty creates history, yet the literature rarely follows that history to its service ending.

# __4. Operating history determines lifetime service__

Operating history turns technical change into service consequence. Road monitoring captures exposure and voltage decline; backup studies resolve parts of the start sequence; stationary studies follow thousands of hours of stack or system performance \[57,58,62,63,66–68\]. The missing link is longitudinal: the same record seldom follows exposure into technical state, then through intervention and downtime to the service finally delivered.

Figure 4 makes this absence visible. Its rows are the anchor studies and its columns are duty or exposure, technical state, maintenance, downtime, replacement, delivered service and retirement. The literature is dense at the beginning of the biography and sparse at the end. Empty cells show where the same asset was no longer followed.

Performance reshaping should be described through quantities that matter to service. Lower efficiency increases hydrogen use for the same delivered output. Reduced available power can limit payload, route, acceleration reserve or protected load. Slower or less reliable start can matter greatly in backup duty. Heat\-to\-power balance can change the useful output of CHP. These statements define consequence pathways rather than universal degradation laws. Quantification requires duty\-matched evidence.

Degradation is likewise plural. A Review centered on lifetime service need not reproduce a component\-by\-component mechanism catalogue. It must instead identify which state changes are observable, whether they are reversible, and how they propagate. Manufacturing studies identify durability and cold\-start capability as practical challenges \[24\], while catalyst\-layer engineering can reduce platinum intensity while raising power density \[70\]. These results do not provide a complete PEMFC mechanism map across the three duties. That missing bridge should remain explicit.

The mobile ribbon shows the first evidence test. Dynamic\-load synthesis explains why transient operation can accelerate ageing \[61\], while road monitoring observes changing degradation across vehicle life \[57\]. Neither yet establishes missed trips, maintenance burden or fleet replacement. Bus roadcalls provide a service\-facing endpoint \[60\], but the available sources do not map individual degradation histories to those events. Closing the ribbon requires these records on compatible vehicles, periods and service denominators.

Mobile lifetime service should therefore be reported as a distribution, not only an average. Completed duty, vehicle availability, hydrogen consumed, maintenance hours, unscheduled removals and stack or balance\-of\-plant replacements can differ across a fleet. The tail matters because a small number of repeatedly unavailable vehicles can disrupt scheduling. California's station evidence adds an upstream service layer: even a healthy fleet can lose mobility when refuelling is inaccessible \[28\]. Stack and supply availability should be separated before they are recombined at the trip endpoint.

The backup ribbon sets a different evidence test. Controlled work establishes that gas admission and load connection form distinct start stages \[62\]. Telecom validation shows that supplementary storage can bridge the transient until the fuel cell accepts more load \[63\]. Simulated cycling shows a possible idling\-temperature effect within an HT\-PEM protocol \[64\]. None establishes successful protection across years of real outages. A complete cohort must connect standby history, demanded start, delivered load, intervention and recovery.

Backup lifetime service is also asymmetric. Many uneventful standby days add little delivered energy, while one failed demand event can be decisive. Energy\-normalized metrics can therefore mislead. The denominator should include qualifying demand opportunities and their consequence, perhaps supplemented by protected\-load hours. This is why no universal score can combine backup with a bus fleet. A low\-capacity\-factor asset can provide high\-value service, but only if readiness is demonstrated.

The stationary ribbon makes accumulation visible. Continuous output allows efficiency and hydrogen\-use trends to be observed over many hours. Planned maintenance can be related to avoided failure, although causal attribution requires an explicit comparison. Unplanned downtime can be weighted by electricity and heat demand at the time. Replacement may restore output but creates a discontinuity in the asset history. A plant\-level lifetime should not reset when the stack is changed if the question concerns the original system's delivered service.

Maintenance is not merely lost time. It can be a strategy that trades a short planned interruption for lower risk of a longer unplanned one. The optimum depends on duty. A depot may coordinate work around dispatch. A backup site may prefer preventive replacement before a critical season. A CHP operator may align service with low heat demand. Evidence should report the rule that triggered maintenance and the service consequence that followed, not only the component changed.

Replacement creates the most important accounting choice. If a stack is replaced, the service system may continue, but manufacturing inputs and downtime have increased. Calling the original stack life the system life ends the observation too early. Calling the system continuously available erases the intervention. Lifetime service retains both facts: cumulative service delivered by the asset and the sequence of replacements required before retirement. This is the bridge from durability to material turnover.

Failure, threshold replacement and retirement do not always coincide. A system may be restored, derated or supported by fallback, so the causal chain is best read from missed service backwards to supply, balance\-of\-plant, stack state and prior exposure. This keeps measurable ageing distinct from actual service loss while preserving maintenance and replacement as turning points in the same life.

Operating history can leave an initial advantage intact, erode it or overturn it. Where comparable service histories are absent, the lifetime comparison remains open.

Figure 4 turns the literature sideways: populated cells cluster around exposure and technical state, while maintenance, downtime, replacement, delivered service and retirement are rarely observed together. The missing middle is therefore a substantive finding, not a lack of more durability numbers.

# __5. Material turnover follows operating history__

Material demand enters the causal spine before operation through manufacturing and re\-enters through replacement. PEMFC membrane\-electrode assemblies depend on platinum catalysts, while stacks and balance\-of\-plant components carry additional manufacturing burdens \[24,26,27,71\]. The relevant lifetime question is not simply how much platinum a new stack contains. It is how much material is installed, repaired or replaced to deliver the duty until the asset retires.

Platinum intensity has fallen through stack and catalyst\-layer design. Toyota reported a 58% reduction in platinum amount per unit output from the first\- to second\-generation Mirai stack \[70\]. The more consequential question is whether a lighter new stack becomes a lighter lifetime. Lower loading reduces initial material, but shorter service life or more replacements can erase part of that gain.

The mobile scene illustrates the arithmetic. A fleet with lower platinum per stack can still accumulate substantial material turnover if high utilization brings earlier replacement or if a subset of vehicles requires repeated intervention. Conversely, a durable high\-utilization stack can spread its initial material across many completed trips. The denominator is cumulative delivered transport service before vehicle retirement, with all stack replacements included. A per\-kilowatt loading alone cannot answer the lifetime question.

The backup scene changes the time basis. A rarely used stack may remain installed for many calendar years and deliver little energy. Material per kilowatt\-hour can look high even if the system successfully protects every demanded event. The more appropriate denominator includes protected\-load service and readiness. Long calendar life can reduce replacement frequency, yet a preventive replacement rule may retire a stack before electrochemical capability is exhausted. That intervention belongs in the lifetime material record.

Continuous stationary duty makes operating\-hour durability more prominent. A stack that delivers electricity and useful heat across many hours can distribute manufacturing inputs over a large service quantity. Efficiency decline may increase hydrogen use before replacement becomes necessary. An operator may replace the stack at an economic threshold rather than physical failure. The material record must therefore be linked to the same state and service history used in the lifetime analysis.

The Review follows material only until the PEMFC asset retires. Initial manufacturing, repair and pre\-retirement replacement belong to the service biography; later recovery and secondary use belong to a separate resource story.

Material turnover also reveals why maintenance data matter. Repair that preserves a stack can extend service without a full replacement, but may require parts and downtime. Refurbishment before retirement can similarly alter the sequence, provided the intervention remains inside the operating asset boundary. Studies should distinguish repair, module replacement and complete stack replacement. Lumping them into “maintenance” hides both service and material consequences.

Figure 5 pairs the reported loading improvement with the missing lifetime record. It asks how much platinum was installed across all replacements for the service delivered before retirement. A loading number without that life is an unfinished sentence.

# __6. Reading the long\-term role of PEMFCs through operating history__

The long\-term role of hydrogen PEMFCs cannot be inferred from an application list or a single durability target. It emerges where a hydrogen starting condition, a service duty and an operating history remain compatible over time. The three scenes show why that compatibility differs. Mobile duty rewards completed high\-utilization service and penalizes disruption. Backup duty rewards readiness during rare demand. Continuous stationary duty rewards sustained output, useful heat and maintainable operation.

For high\-frequency mobile systems, road monitoring now establishes that real operating histories can yield non\-linear degradation trajectories \[57,58\]. Transit studies add operation, maintenance and roadcall concepts \[59,60\], while supply records show that refuelling access can independently constrain mobility \[28\]. Within the assembled evidence, the absent connection is a common dataset joining each vehicle's route exposure, diagnosed state, intervention, availability and completed transport. Until then, mobile lifetime service remains application\- and infrastructure\-specific.

For low\-utilization high\-reliability backup, experiments and field\-facing validation describe start\-up and transient load takeover \[62,63\]. The central uncertainty is the conversion of elapsed time into readiness. Quiet years can preserve a low operating\-hour count while ageing fuel, auxiliaries and interfaces; repeated tests can create hours without recreating the outage that gives the system meaning. The decisive record is therefore not energy output alone, but whether calendar exposure culminated in protected\-load delivery when demand arrived.

For stationary and CHP duty, long tests and models reveal how output and fuel use evolve over thousands of hours \[66–68\]. Their larger contribution is to show that endurance alone does not equal accepted electricity, useful heat or reliable site service. The lifetime ending appears only when operating trajectories, interventions and delivered outputs share one record.

Across all duties, operating history gives efficiency and stack life a provenance. A system receives hydrogen, attempts a duty, accumulates exposure, changes state, is interrupted or maintained, and eventually retires. The shared form is a biography; the ending remains duty\-specific.

The comparison then asks what happened to an initial advantage within the same duty. It may endure, narrow or reverse as supply disruptions, efficiency loss, downtime and replacement accumulate. Transfer to another site depends on causal resemblance—route and refuelling for mobility, standby environment and event duration for backup, fuel, load and heat demand for CHP—rather than on a universal average lifetime.

This discipline also changes research priorities. More rated\-performance records will not close the central gap by themselves. The informative study follows a system through enough history to observe exposure, state, intervention and service on one timeline. It reports negative outcomes, including missed service and unsuccessful maintenance, because these events reveal causal links. It preserves alternative explanations by separating stack, balance\-of\-plant, supply and external causes.

The three biographies demand different scales of attention. Mobility packs exposure into crowded days but still needs enough seasons to reveal change. Backup power stretches evidence across quiet years because the decisive event is rare. Stationary duty accumulates hours steadily, yet maintenance and replacement arrive on a calendar of their own. A study becomes decisive when it follows the relevant clock long enough to witness a real intervention and its service consequence—not because every field is filled, but because change in technical state and change in lived service are finally seen together.

Negative events are not untidy records at the edge of a success story; they are often its most revealing plot turns. A failed start, a substitute vehicle, a winter repair or an early stack change shows where rated advantage met the world. Preserving such moments prevents the smooth histories of survivors from standing in for the lives of entire fleets and sites.

Figure 6 gives the Review a comparative ending. One rated advantage enters three lives assembled from field records, controlled tests, models and still\-open links. It may endure, narrow, reverse or remain unresolved. The figure assigns no duty to an outcome in advance. Its value lies in showing why rated performance cannot determine a lifetime comparison before the life has been observed.

Two bounded examples show why service must remain visible. California's public refuelling network combined apparent statewide capacity with uneven availability and local access, while Lower Saxony later favoured battery trains on routes where charging opportunities and future electrification changed the comparison \[28,29\]. Together they show that installed capacity and an application label cannot decide the service outcome.

The synthesis also keeps alternatives visible. A PEMFC may be a credible choice where rapid refuelling, high utilization, duration, grid constraint or a chemical storage function has service value \[8,10,20,23,76\]. Direct electricity, batteries, catenary or another demonstrated fuel may better serve other duties. A fair lifetime comparison applies the same delivered\-service and reliability boundary to every option. Hydrogen losing a bounded comparison is an informative result, not a failure of the technology.

Co\-located, distributed, regional and corridor arrangements solve different supply and service problems \[8,43–53\]. Location matters through delivered\-hydrogen burden, reliability, utilization, maintenance access and the service being attempted; it remains one determinant of operating history rather than the article's final answer.

The strongest practical reading rule is simple. Do not ask first how long a stack lasts. Ask what service was attempted, under what supply and duty, what operating history accumulated, what interventions occurred, and what service remained before retirement. Stack life then becomes one component of a traceable explanation. This ordering prevents an hour target from being mistaken for a lifetime outcome.

# __Conclusion__

Hydrogen PEMFC systems do not possess durability in the abstract. Supply conditions, duty, environment and intervention write distinct lives: a crowded mobile day, a long wait for a rare outage, or the steady accumulation of electricity and useful heat. Downtime, repair and replacement are part of the service delivered.

The literature is rich in new\-stack performance and terminal durability values, yet much thinner in the middle where exposure becomes state change, intervention and service consequence. Following that missing middle turns durability from a component property into a system history.

An initial advantage may survive, narrow or reverse as the system lives. The decisive question is therefore not how long a stack can run, but how a hydrogen PEMFC system kept—or failed to keep—its promise across the service life it actually lived.

# __Figure storyboards and draft captions__

## __Graphical Abstract | One technology, three service biographies__

The same new PEMFC system enters three distinct lives. Mobile duty compresses transients, refuelling dependence and repair into crowded working days; backup duty stretches across quiet calendar time and concentrates value in rare demand events; stationary and CHP duty accumulates operating hours, fuel use and heat–power trade\-offs. Supply conditions set the opening position, while maintenance, downtime and replacement reshape each life before retirement. The graphic ends with three service outcomes—transport completed, critical load protected, and electricity plus useful heat accepted—without combining them into a universal score.

## __Figure 1 | From rated capability to lifetime service__

A continuous causal map links delivered\-hydrogen conditions to duty, exposure, changing system state, intervention and service before retirement. A side branch shows hydrogen supply returning later as shortage, delay, off\-specification fuel or unavailable dispensing. The figure distinguishes stack state, balance\-of\-plant state and service outcome, then records attempted, completed and missed service on the same asset timeline. The central message is that rated capability becomes valuable only after it crosses the system boundary and survives the life written by duty.

## __Figure 2 | Hydrogen supply sets the starting conditions and can interrupt the service later__

The supply path is summarized through carbon and water intensity, purity, pressure, conditioning loss, usable reserve, distance, throughput and accessibility. Three small scenes show those conditions meeting a mobile departure, a rare backup outage and continuous stationary demand. Selected literature values may be used as bounded anchors—for example, 20\-to\-600\-bar compression at 1.51 and 1.77 kWh kg−1 H2 under the cited model, and Shanghai pathway costs above 50 or below 40 CNY kg−1 H2 under their local assumptions. The visual message is that supply changes the opening advantage and can later remove service even when the stack remains healthy.

## __Figure 3 | Three duties write three incomplete operating histories__

Three horizontal time ribbons preserve the native clocks and units of mobile, backup and stationary/CHP service. The mobile ribbon includes road monitoring of more than 100 vehicles, a bus degradation record and controlled cycling; the backup ribbon includes start\-up response and telecom validation; the stationary ribbon includes long\-duration stack tests and a 15,000\-hour CHP model. Each ribbon records exposure and technical state, then ends in a deliberate visual gap before maintenance, replacement or service outcome. The gap is the finding: strong fragments exist, but the same asset is rarely followed through the entire service biography.

## __Figure 4 | The missing middle in PEMFC lifetime evidence__

Rows represent the anchor studies; columns represent duty or exposure, technical state, maintenance, downtime, replacement, delivered service and retirement. Filled cells show what the same record actually follows. Empty cells show where observation stops. A companion panel lists the three service endings—completed transport, protected\-load events, and accepted electricity plus useful heat—while calendar age, operating hours and event counts appear as descriptive clocks rather than substitute endpoints. The figure turns a diffuse literature gap into a visible pattern: the evidence is dense near the beginning of life and sparse where technical change becomes lived service.

## __Figure 5 | A lighter stack is not automatically a lighter lifetime__

The first panel shows the reported reduction in platinum amount per unit output between the first\- and second\-generation Mirai stacks, normalized from 1.00 to 0.42. The second panel places loading, service\-preserving life, repair and complete stack replacement on the same asset timeline. An optional transparent sensitivity may show 0.42, 0.84 and 1.26 relative installed\-platinum units after zero, one and two full replacements; these are arithmetic illustrations, not observed replacement probabilities. The final panel defines the missing lifetime quantity as cumulative platinum installed before retirement divided by duty\-specific service delivered.

## __Figure 6 | How an initial advantage survives, narrows or reverses__

The final synthesis follows one initial PEMFC advantage through three service biographies assembled from field records, controlled tests, models and still\-open links. Four endings are shown in plain language: the advantage survives; it narrows; it reverses relative to a duty\-matched alternative; or the lifetime comparison remains unresolved because the relevant service history was never observed. California refuelling access and Lower Saxony rail procurement can appear as bounded examples of how capacity and application labels fail to determine service outcomes. The figure closes the Review with a change in viewpoint: rated performance starts the comparison, but lived service decides it.
